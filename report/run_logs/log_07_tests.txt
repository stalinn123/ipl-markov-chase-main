[CONCLUSION - Time Homogeneity]:
The hypothesis of strict time-homogeneity is statistically rejected (p < 0.001).
In physical cricket terms, transition probabilities shift systematically as the chase progresses:
- In the Powerplay (overs 1-6), field restrictions encourage boundary hitting with low wicket risk,
  leading to transient state persistence.
- In the Death overs (overs 16-20), absorbing transitions surge drastically: batting teams either rapidly
  reach the target (absorbing into WIN) or lose lower-order wickets slogging (absorbing into LOSS).
While non-stationary phase models can capture fine-grained phase pacing, the stationary model provides
a remarkably strong parsimonious baseline that accurately captures the aggregate macro-dynamics.

[CONCLUSION - Markov Property]:
The strict memorylessness assumption (first-order Markov chain) is statistically rejected
in favor of a second-order chain (G^2 = 1445.27, df = 190, p < 0.001).
In cricket, this reflects 'match momentum' and multi-over spells:
- A team that lost 2 wickets in over k-1 is psychologically and tactically constrained in over k,
  even if the nominal state (wickets bucket and pressure bucket) appears identical to a team
  that had a tranquil previous over.
- Bowler spell continuity and batter partnership rhythm introduce short-term autocorrelation.
Nonetheless, the state definitions (wickets lost, CRR, RRR, and pressure) encode the cumulative
history sufficiently well that the first-order Markov chain achieves high out-of-sample accuracy (75.2%)
without suffering from the curse of dimensionality or data sparsity that plagues higher-order models.
